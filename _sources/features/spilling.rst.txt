##############################
Spilling and functional syntax
##############################

A rule that threads a value from one call straight into the next has to name
that value, and a name like ``Tmp`` or ``Aux1`` carries no information: it is
there only to plumb the two calls together. Spilling removes the need for that name.

.. code-block:: elpi

   pred f int -> int.
   f X R :- foo X Tmp, bar Tmp R.  % the plumbing variable Tmp

Wrapping a partially-applied call in braces, ``{ E }`` (:doc:`../syntax/terms`),
lifts the goal ``E`` out to just before the smallest enclosing predicate call
and puts a fresh variable in its place, one for each argument ``E`` is not
given (its output arguments, so one in the common case):

.. code-block:: elpi

   f X R :- bar {foo X} R.         % the same, spilled

If ``E`` has several missing arguments, ``{ E }`` becomes that many variables
at once:

.. code-block:: elpi

   pred h int -> int, int.
   g X R :- h X Tmp1 Tmp2, k Tmp1 Tmp2 R.   % h has two outputs: Tmp1 Tmp2
   g X R :- k {h X} R.                      % {h X} stands for the two of them

For this to work Elpi has to know the spilled predicate's arity, from its
``pred`` / ``func`` signature (:doc:`../syntax/type-declarations`); an
undeclared predicate cannot be spilled.

The spilled call is inserted just before the *closest* call recognised as a
predicate, walking backward, inside out. ``elpi -print FILE`` prints a program
after spilling (and other compilation passes), so any doubt about where a
particular ``{ }`` ended up can be checked directly.

Here ``{std.rev L}`` becomes a fresh variable produced by an :stdlib:`std.rev` goal
inserted right before :stdlib:`std.append`:

Spilling in the head of a rule
==============================

``{ }`` may also appear in a rule's head even though a head is not a goal
sequence and cannot simply join a ``,`` conjunction the way a spill in the body can.
The spilled goal is relocated to
just after ``:-``, in front of whatever body the rule already had;:

.. code-block:: elpi

   func copy tm -> tm.
   copy (app A B) (app {copy A} {copy B}).
   % becomes
   copy (app A B) (app R S) :- copy A R, copy B S.


Spilling in the head is restricted to an output argument.

Spilling without ``{ }``
=========================

``{ }`` marks explicitly *which* under-applied call to spill. The
``:autospill`` rule attribute (:doc:`../syntax/rule-attributes`) spills
*every* under-applied call in the rule instead, wherever it appears, exactly
as if each had its own ``{ }``:

.. code-block:: elpi

   :autospill
   main :- std.append (std.append [1] [2]) [3] L, L = [1,2,3].

A call is spilled automatically when it is **applied to all of its input
arguments but none of its outputs** arguments. A call that already supplies
every argument, outputs included, is left untouched -- and so is a call
already surrounded by ``{ }``, even though it too supplies all its inputs
and no outputs.

The ``:autospill`` attribute is **turned on automatically** by the functional
rule syntax (:doc:`../syntax/inference-rules-and-queries`) — a rule head
written with ``->`` to separate inputs from outputs. A recursive call is
the typical beneficiary, since its own result is exactly the value still
missing from it: below, ``map F XS`` stands for its own missing output:

Spilling into a binder
======================

Under ``pi`` and in the conclusion of ``==>``, the spilled goal is placed
*inside* the binder, so its fresh variable can still mention the names and
hypotheses in scope there. A pretty-printer that recurses under ``lam`` spills
``{pp (F x)}`` right where the fresh ``x`` and its ``pp x "x"`` hypothesis are
visible:

**../code/spilling-under-binder.elpi:**

.. literalinclude:: ../code/spilling-under-binder.elpi
   :linenos:
   :language: elpi

.. code-block:: console

   \x.(x x)
   

``elpi -print FILE`` shows where the spilled goal landed: in the ``lam`` rule
it sits after the ``==>``, inside the ``pi`` and under its own ``sigma``,
equivalent to writing

.. code-block:: elpi

   pp (lam F) S :- pi x\ pp x "x" ==> sigma R\ (pp (F x) R, S is "\x." ^ R).

``{ }`` may spill in the premise (the hypothesis) of ``==>`` too.
Spilling in the premise is handled the same way
as spilling in a rule's own head (above) and the premise becomes a rule with premises
of its own and the fresh variable used for spilling is quantified with ``pi`` around the entire premise:

.. code-block:: elpi

   main :- p {succ 1} ==> p X, print X.

is elaborated to:   
   
.. code-block:: elpi

   main :- (pi R\ p R :- succ 1 R) ==> p X, print X.



Spilling out of a term-level lambda
===================================

A ``{ }`` inside a lambda that is passed as *data*, not the body of a ``pi``
or an implication, goes the other way: the goal is lifted *out* past the
lambda and the fresh variable is η-expanded so it can still depend on the
bound name. ``apply (x\ {f x})`` becomes

.. code-block:: elpi

   main :- apply (x\ {f x}).
   % becomes
   main :- pi c\ f c (R c), apply (x\ R x).

The spilled ``f c`` runs once, before ``apply``, with ``c`` a fresh constant.
This is only meaningful when ``f`` does not actually need ``c``'s value; a
``{ }`` under a term-level lambda that genuinely depends on the bound variable
is usually a mistake.


Spilling a conjunction
======================

When ``{ }`` wraps a conjunction, only its *last* conjunct is spilled, and
that conjunct's missing output is what becomes the fresh variable. The earlier
conjuncts run in place, typically to bind a variable the last one then reads:

.. code-block:: elpi

   foo :- pi x\ f {g x Y, h Y}.
   % becomes
   foo :- pi x\ sigma S\ (g x Y, h Y S), f S.

``g x Y`` runs first and binds ``Y``; ``h Y`` is the spilled conjunct, so
``h Y S`` computes the value ``S`` that takes the place of the whole
``{ … }``.

**Related work.** Similar transformations exist in other systems under different
names, although none of them handles lambda-tree syntax. Here some pointers:

- Picat replaces a function call ``f(t1,...,tn)`` by a new variable ``V``, and
  inserts a new predicate call ``p(t1,...,tn,V)`` before the goal in which the
  function call occurs (:ref:`Zhou, PADL 2017 <bib-picat>`).
- Curry's intermediate representation, FlatCurry, normalizes nested function
  calls the same way via let-insertion (``y:ins(x,ys)`` becomes
  ``let {z = ins(x,ys)} in y:z``) (:ref:`Antoy, Hanus, Jost & Libby, Declare
  2019 <bib-icurry>`).
- A-normal form, the general functional-compiler ancestor of this family:
  flatten nested expressions by let-binding every intermediate result
  (:ref:`Sabry & Felleisen, LFP 1992 <bib-anf-lfp>`; :ref:`Flanagan, Sabry,
  Duba & Felleisen, PLDI 1993 <bib-anf-pldi>`).
- Prolog's CLP(FD) flattens arithmetic expressions the same way, scoped to
  arithmetic: ``X #= Y+Z*W`` compiles to a sequence of primitive constraint
  goals over fresh variables via ``goal_expansion/2`` (:ref:`Triska, FLOPS
  2012 <bib-clpfd>`).
